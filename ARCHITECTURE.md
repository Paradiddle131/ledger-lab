# Architecture

The current design, and each major choice with its reason and what it rejected.
Rewritten in place: it describes the system as it is now.

## Components and seams

Real public documents are fetched, landed and loaded into the warehouse; an open-source ERP posts them into journal lines; layered models turn both into certified data products; governed metrics sit on top; one agent answers from them and another drafts entries a named human approves.
A control plane (identity, tool policy, approval, guardrails, audit, traces, evals, cost caps) spans every agent call.

| Component | Runs on | Seam | State |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ingest/`: download scripts with checksums, landing | the session, then Cloud Storage | a landing path from the environment (`LEDGER_LAB_LANDING`, default `landing/`); sources recorded in [`ingest/sources.yaml`](ingest/sources.yaml) | fetch-and-verify kit built and tested against a local server (`python -m ingest.fetch <source>`, or a set's name for every source it prefixes, landed together by the set's step); retrying a download whose connection drops; six sources recorded and landed: Chicago's contracts and payments (`ingest/chicago/`), as one set (`chicago`) whose step scrubs both tables together, through one shared step that hashes every vendor or payee who may be a person with the pseudonymiser ([`ingest/individuals.py`](ingest/individuals.py)) and keeps in clear the names the landed legal-entity register vouches for ([`ingest/entities.py`](ingest/entities.py)), so it refuses to run until the register is landed, the ECB's daily euro reference rates for the pound and the US dollar (`ingest/ecb/`), the Dutch central government's budget statements for every year the publisher serves, one row per budget moment (`ingest/nl_government/`), and the GLEIF register's records for the vendors' countries, without what may name a person (`ingest/gleif/`, whose step uses the pseudonymiser's classifier to leave such records out and hashes nothing), and the UK Department for Transport's spending over £25,000 for fiscal year 2025, its twelve monthly files as one set in one shape, through a step that hashes every supplier who may be a person with the pseudonymiser and the register's vouching alike (`ingest/uk_transport/`); the ECB and Dutch steps name no one; the kit refuses a source that names people until it has such a step; the Cloud Storage landing bucket declared in [`platform/gcp/`](platform/gcp) and validated offline, not applied |

The catalogues are YAML validated by a JSON Schema beside each file, and `tests/` checks their cross-references: every grant names a known persona, entity and product, every control's owner and every glossary term's owner is a human persona, a glossary term's references name other terms, and a control is marked implemented only once every one of its proving tests exists.

## Choices

- **Real public data, not generated data:** real records carry the revisions, negative awards, missing identifiers and revised figures that generated data leaves out, and those are what the controls must survive; rejected: generated data (clean by construction, so it proves nothing about mess), a well-known marketplace dataset (its non-commercial licence would bind everything derived from it).
- **Three public entities as the row boundary: City of Chicago (USD), UK Department for Transport (GBP), Dutch central government (EUR):** Chicago brings the only public contract to voucher to payment chain, the UK a second file layout and an April to March year, the Netherlands budget against outturn; together they give three currencies and three fiscal calendars to translate and consolidate; rejected: one entity (no row boundary, no translation), a private company's ledger (none is public).
- **Reference data from its publishers:** ECB reference rates translate currency, the GLEIF register matches vendor names to legal entities, and annual filings from the securities regulator give an independent figure to tie out against; rejected: a public warehouse copy of the filings (stale for years).
- **An open-source ERP (Odoo) as posting engine and write target:** it posts the real documents into journal lines by real accounting rules, loaded as SAP-shaped ledger tables, and is the system the acting agent drafts into; rejected: hand-written posting rules (accounting the lab invents), a vendor ERP (licensed and not free).
- **DuckDB through dbt-duckdb as the local engine until BigQuery takes over:** it runs in process with nothing to start, its dialect is the closest that works to BigQuery's (`QUALIFY`, structs and lists, window functions), and the switch is the dbt profile's target, which `LEDGER_LAB_GCP_PROJECT` selects, with the BigQuery adapter in its own dependency group; DuckDB enforces no row, column or masking rules, so the local access harness enforces them itself and the BigQuery proof stays the real one; dialect gaps are cast away in the model SQL (DuckDB's date truncation returns a timestamp, BigQuery's a date); rejected: a BigQuery emulator (partial dialect coverage, fragile under dbt, and it emulates none of the row and column rules), PostgreSQL (real row security and roles, but the farthest dialect and the largest rewrite at the switch).
- **Fiscal calendars as a period table and a day table built from a seed of each entity's first month, over the fiscal years one dbt var (`fiscal_years`) names, with period status as a seed of one row per period:** each year is twelve calendar months from the entity's first month (January for Chicago and the Dutch government, April for the UK department), labelled by the calendar year it opens in (the UK's 2024-25 is 2024) and numbered from period 1, so a day falls in one period and later models join on entity, year and period; dbt data tests hold the rules (one period per day, contiguous periods, the UK year opening on 1 April and closing on 31 March, a status for every period); a date outside the covered years has no row, so a later model tests each date column with the project's `dates_in_fiscal_calendar` test, which fails where a join would silently drop the row; and dbt-core's own cross-database date spine and date macros keep the SQL unchanged for BigQuery; the Dutch budget moments are versions of one year's budget, not periods a day falls in, so the calendar leaves them out and a budget-against-outturn model will carry its moment beside the year; rejected: labelling a year by the year it closes in (the UK writes 2024-25, opening year first), dbt-utils for the date spine (a package download for what dbt-core ships), a case per entity in the SQL (a new entity would be a code change, not a row).
- **Currency is translated by the `translate_currency` macro through the ECB's euro rates, one rate set a day in `fx_rates`: the set of the posting or payment date, the previous business day's when that day is closed (carried at most four days), the cross rate through the euro, or with `constant_rates_on` one named day's set for every row (constant currency); results round once, half away from zero, to two decimal places, and an amount no rate translates stops the query:** the ECB publishes each currency against the euro only, so dollars to pounds is the amount divided by dollars per euro and multiplied by pounds per euro, rounded once so it never drifts a penny from its two legs; `fx_rates` spreads each publication date over the days before the next, so its days follow the published rates whatever years the calendars cover; four days is the longest run of closed days in the published rates (Good Friday to Easter Monday, or 25 and 26 December beside a weekend), so a longer gap, a day before the first rate or after the last, and an unknown or missing currency have no rate, and the macro raises an error naming the currencies and the day (both engines spell it `error(message)` and evaluate it only on the row a `case` sends to it), since a null would vanish from a sum and a caller could forget its test; a null amount stays null; rates are decimals of six places and amounts dbt's `type_numeric` (`numeric(28,6)` on DuckDB), and because DuckDB divides decimals as floats, which misround a half cent (542.05 dollars on 5 April 2024 are exactly 428.865 pounds), the DuckDB quotient is taken exactly in 128-bit integers, while BigQuery divides in BIGNUMERIC, rounding to cents from 38 decimal places (not yet run on BigQuery); the `ecb_rates` var reads the landed file by default, so a run with nothing landed fails on the missing file, and the builds in the tests pass `sample` to read a committed fortnight of real rates (25 March to 8 April 2024: a weekend, and Good Friday to Easter Monday; the ECB allows free use with the source cited); rejected: rounding the euro leg first (a cent's drift from the cross), carrying a rate with no limit (a missing week would reuse a stale rate silently), a null for a missing rate with a not_null test each caller adds (a forgotten test lets a sum skip the row), dividing BigQuery's NUMERIC (rounded to 9 places, then to cents, it can round twice near a half cent for amounts with more than 2 decimal places), a day spine from the fiscal calendars (rates outside their years would silently get no row), a package for the fill (a window and a five-row offset in dbt-core do it), the sample as the default (a real run would quietly translate with test fixtures), rates built at test time from a download (`make check` would need the network).
- **Native GCP services only where free, a stand-in behind the same seam otherwise:** BigQuery enforces row, column and masking rules itself, which is the point; the platform's catalog, lineage and native data agent are used only once they prove free; rejected: stand-ins for everything (they prove nothing about the real platform's enforcement), paid managed services (the lab spends $0.00).
- **Personas as service identities that tests impersonate, with a mapping table in place of a group directory:** each persona id is a service account id, `platform/gcp/` declares one account per persona read from `access/personas.yaml` itself, with no project role but the one that runs a query, and the agent account may mint tokens on each of them and nowhere else; a test acts as a persona and asserts what the warehouse returns, on BigQuery by impersonating `<persona>@<project>.iam.gserviceaccount.com`, and the persona to entity to data product table in `access/` plays the directory's role; rejected: a group directory (needs an organisation and an administrator the lab does not have), personal accounts (need real people and email addresses in a public repo).
- **A persona on DuckDB is a database file named after it, opened read-only with no other file reachable, no extension beyond DuckDB's built-in ones, and its configuration locked:** DuckDB has no users or roles, so the identity is the catalog the connection opens, which it cannot rename or leave, and what that catalog holds: a copy of only the products a `read` or `edit` grant names, and of an entity-scoped product (its entity id in an `entity` column) only the granted entities' rows, so asking for anything else finds no table; an `aggregate` grant reaches no rows until the minimum-group rule exists, and `draft` and `post` read nothing in the warehouse, since the proposal and approval workflow carries the entry; DuckDB lets every connection create temporary objects that shadow unqualified names, so a persona's reach is read through qualified names (`system.main.current_database()`, `"<persona>".main.<product>`), and a shadow can forge only what its own connection shows under an unqualified name, never reach another product; rejected: views or a filter over the shared warehouse (a connection that can open the warehouse reads past them), a persona label the test honours (a temporary macro overrides it).
- **The warehouse's access rules as OpenTofu beside the fences, generated from the catalogues, for one owner apply:** `platform/gcp/warehouse.tf` declares a dataset per data product and per model layer no persona reads (`landed` for load jobs, `intermediate` for the layers between), all in the fences' region, where the landing bucket is, so load jobs read it in place and the policy tags and masking rules, which must share the tables' location, sit beside them; the agent holds a custom builder role on those datasets only, data editor's table and data permissions, the datasets' routines, every row access policy permission (creating, listing and deleting them and setting their grantees) and setting a policy tag on a column, but neither dataset nor table IAM nor deleting a dataset, and a test holds that it has none of those and no fence-moving permission, each drawn from data owner's recorded list; setting a policy tag also needs reading the taxonomy, which the agent is granted on the taxonomy itself; a persona's `read` grant becomes data viewer and `edit` data editor on the product's dataset, `aggregate`, `draft` and `post` nothing, and a persona with either holds one project role, custom, that only creates jobs and reads BigQuery's settings; until a table carries its row access policies a dataset read reaches all its rows, so the models create each entity-scoped table with its policies; so the dbt profile's location (`LEDGER_LAB_BQ_LOCATION`) must name that region; rejected: the US multi-region (whether a load job reads a regional bucket into it could not be confirmed offline, and the taxonomy and masking rules would have to follow it), one dataset with a table grant per product (the tables do not exist until the models build them), data owner on the datasets (it also sets their IAM, so the agent could grant anyone, everyone on the internet included, any role there, and delete them), data owner or data editor on the project (it would reach every dataset, the billing export's included), the predefined job user role (it also opens Gemini, Dataform and conversational analytics, not all of them free).
- **Policy tags as data in `access/columns.yaml`, each with one of BigQuery's built-in masking rules and its readers listed, and row access policies as SQL in the models:** each tag names what it marks, its masking rule (nullify, hash, last four) and the personas that read it in clear and masked, and a test holds that every persona that reads the warehouse is exactly one of the two for every tag, so no query it may run is refused on a column, while one that reads nothing is neither; `platform/gcp/columns.tf` declares one taxonomy, a policy tag and a masking rule per tag, fine-grained reader on the tag for the clear readers and masked reader on the rule for the masked ones, and the agent reads every tag in clear, since a model copying a masked value would store the mask; the taxonomy and its tags refuse to be destroyed by an apply, since a deleted tag can leave its columns unprotected, so renaming a tag is the owner's hand change; the mapping is here, not in `access/personas.yaml`, because it is about columns, which the column list will join when the payments contract exists; row access policies attach to tables the models create, so they stay in the models; rejected: the mapping in `access/personas.yaml` (it holds products, not columns), listing only clear readers and deriving the masked (a reader left out of both would be refused silently), custom masking routines (their availability on on-demand pricing is unclear), row access policies in OpenTofu (the tables do not exist when it applies).
- **Orchestration in plain containers, not Kubernetes:** Airflow, the ERP and their shared database fit one cloud session, and a cluster would teach nothing this lab is for; rejected: a managed cluster (not free), a local cluster (spends the session's memory on plumbing).
- **The native data agent first, an own agent as the comparison:** the native agent runs on the asking person's credentials, so the warehouse's rules apply unchanged; an own agent over the same metrics measures right answers, confident wrong answers, cost and auditability against it; rejected: an own agent only (rebuilds what the platform owns), the native agent only (no yardstick).
- **One Compose file with a profile per service for the container stack:** the shared PostgreSQL 16 server is the only service without a profile and always starts, and the ERP, lineage backend, orchestrator and tracing tool each join under a profile named after the service, so a session runs only what its item needs from one entry point (`make stack-up PROFILE=<service>`), and a fresh clone reproduces it; the image's init-script folder creates one database per planned service (ERP, lineage backend, orchestrator, agent session store, workflow checkpointer) on first start, from one list in the Compose file that the live test also reads, and the healthcheck asks over TCP because the first-start server answers only on its socket until those scripts finish; the password comes from `LEDGER_LAB_DB_PASSWORD`, else from a file `make stack-up` generates and git ignores; each checkout is its own Compose project on a free loopback port, so checkouts sharing a Docker daemon never meet; `make stack-down` removes the volume and the generated password with the containers, because init scripts run only on an empty volume and the stack holds nothing a script cannot rebuild; rejected: one Compose file per service with its own database (more files to start and a database server each, spending the session's memory), the session's preinstalled PostgreSQL (cloud-only, so a clone cannot reproduce it), a volume kept across `make stack-down` (a changed database list or password would silently not apply), a fixed host port and project name (sibling checkouts on one daemon would fight over them).
- **A vendor or payee is an organisation only on evidence, and every other name becomes a keyed HMAC-SHA-256 (`ingest/individuals.py`, key `LEDGER_LAB_HASH_KEY`):** the evidence is a name the caller knows is an organisation, or a legal form of a separate legal person (final UK, Dutch or US forms, leading Dutch foundation, association or cooperative forms) whose remaining words are one plain party, with no joiner of a second party (a comma except one directly before the form, a dash, slash, bracket, dot or `&`, a word such as `and`, `as` or `c/o`, a title, a person's suffix, a role such as executor or guardian, a trust) and, before `B.V.` or `N.V.`, which also read as a surname's initials, two words that are neither single letters nor name particles, while a trading-as or also-known-as part, an estate, executors or a deceased overrides even the register; the failure that matters is a person in clear, so sole traders and partnerships, which are no separate legal person, are hashed, and so are real companies with a joined name (`Doe & Roe Ltd`, `Acme (UK) Ltd`, `Rolls-Example Ltd`, `Dr Example Drinks Ltd`) until the register vouches for them, while a company named after a person with nothing joined (`Jane Doe Ltd`) passes in clear on purpose, being a legal person of its own; a person becomes `person:` and the 64-digit hex HMAC of the name after NFKC, straight quotes, invisible format characters dropped, case folding, whitespace collapsed and edge punctuation stripped, a missing payee stays missing, and the pseudonymiser refuses to start without a key of at least 32 characters (the classifier alone needs none); rejected: a person-name detector (it must know every name of every culture, and its misses are leaks), an unkeyed hash (a list of candidate names reverses it), a default or file-held key (anyone with the tree could reverse the hashes).
- **The legal-entity register vouches that a payee is an organisation only for the whole name of a separate legal person, and never for a person-shaped name (`ingest/entities.py`):** a payee the classifier would hash lands in clear when a landed register record has the same matching key (the pseudonymiser's normalisation, punctuation between words ignored, `&` kept as a word), a legal-form code on an explicit list of separate legal persons (every British and Dutch body corporate form, and the American corporation, company and bank forms the pinned register files a vouching record under; no partnership but a British LLP, no trust, no unincorporated association, no British government entity), a category the landing keeps, no person marker in the payee's name, and more than three words or a word that is not plain letters; the steps that hash read the landed register and refuse without it, and the model's looser matching never decides what is hashed; on the pinned files this keeps 46 contract vendors (3,715 rows), 87 payees (9,858 rows) and 41 UK suppliers (712 rows) in clear and refuses 33, 81 and 13 exact matches for being person-shaped, because a sole trader may share a name with a registered company and nothing in the record tells them apart; rejected: stripping the register's legal form (`Example Ltd` vouching for `Example`: on the pinned files it adds 418 names, four in five of them one to three words, the shape of a person sharing a company's name), legal-form synonyms (`Limited` for `Ltd`: 63 more names across the three files, and a payee's own legal form already makes most such names organisations), vouching for person-shaped exact matches (127 more names, and the failure that matters is a person in clear), counting four words as person-shaped too (it keeps a further 47 names hashed), any real legal-form code (partnerships and trusts are usually named after people), a register read from another source's landing without refusing when it is missing (the result would depend silently on what happens to be landed), writing an LEI beside a hashed name (the register is public, so it would name the person).
- **`vendor_entity` matches each landed vendor name to a legal entity by three rules, first match deciding: the exact matching key in any country (score 1.0), the same name before the same legal form however spelt (0.9), and the same name before any or no legal form (0.7), the looser two only within the entity's own country:** of a rule's candidates the entity's own country and then active records are preferred, and a name matches only when the preferred candidates are one legal entity, `ambiguous` otherwise; the keys are dbt macros (`models/macros/vendor_match.sql`) whose exact key a test holds to the landing's; a hashed name is never matched and carries no LEI; a labelled sample of 117 public companies' names, 81 with the LEI they name (23 of them in clear only on the register's word), with every register record the rules could consider for them (`tests/models/vendor_sample.yaml`), holds the precision at 0.95 or more in `make check` and on the real landing; rejected: fuzzy similarity scores (a near name is often a sister company, and a score per pair needs a blocking key anyway), a dbt Python model (BigQuery runs those only on a paid service), matching on the headquarters address (not landed).
- **One fetch kit verifies every source in memory before writing, with a per-source step between:** `ingest/fetch.py` streams the bytes the publisher serves into memory, undecoded (it asks for no content encoding, so the hash is the one a published checksum gives), compares their SHA-256 with the one recorded in `ingest/sources.yaml`, and only then hands them to the source's own step (`STEPS`, by source name) or, with none, writes them as they are, through a temporary file and a rename that leaves nothing behind on failure; an entry must say whether it names people, and one that does is refused before any request until it has a step, so unverified bytes never reach the landing path and a source that names people never has its raw bytes on disk; a failing step is reported by source and kind only, since its message could quote names; the cost is a source bounded by memory, so a response over 512 MiB or still arriving after 30 minutes fails; rejected: streaming to a temporary file and hashing as it lands (raw bytes, names included, would touch disk), a script per source (each would re-implement the verification).
- **A source that names people lands as text Parquet, built in memory, with every possibly-personal payee hashed and every cell that could name them blanked or left out:** Chicago's contracts and payments land together as one set (`python -m ingest.fetch chicago`, `ingest/chicago/together.py`), through one shared step that scrubs both tables at once (`ingest/chicago/table.py`, configured in `contracts.py` and `payments.py`), so nothing lands until both have verified and neither lands alone; a payee is hashed exactly when the pseudonymiser finds no evidence of an organisation, and a vendor id is hashed (`id:`, keyed, still a join key) exactly on the rows whose name is, so an id that carries a person-like and a company name stays in clear beside the company and hashed beside the person, and a name is hashed in both tables or in neither; a hashed vendor's rows lose their description, ZIP code and contract PDF link; the street address columns never land, since they carry contact people (`ATTN`, `C/O`) and nothing needs them; a description on any row is blanked when a run of its words, letters and digits in any order and punctuation, is a name either table hashes (a contract description naming someone the City paid only by voucher included), or when it introduces a person by a marker (the estate or executors of someone, a deceased, a name that is no company trading as another; a marker word alone, such as an office estate, no longer counts in a description), and an organisation row's description is otherwise landed as served, the remaining place a person's name could sit unseen; on the pinned files the shared scrub blanks 1,315 contract descriptions more than each table's own scrub did, for names only payments hash (the landed tests' own reading finds 26 such names), and the landed test holds that count to zero in both directions; payments have no id, address or description, and their step refuses a voucher or contract number that is not a code (a digit and no space, or the placeholders `DV` and `Multiple`); contract, specification and voucher numbers stay in clear as the keys between the tables, so a contract whose rows carry both a hashed and a clear name links the two, and the City's public search can look a hashed payee up by them; the rows never touch disk (an in-memory filesystem between the CSV and DuckDB's Parquet writer, with DuckDB's spilling off, in `ingest/scrub.py`, which the UK step shares); every column stays the publisher's text and typing is the models' job; the landed tests (`pytest -m landed`, left out of `make check`, run with the landing key) profile each file and hash every run of words of every clear cell, read by their own tokenising, as a person's name: they assert that no description or code holds a name either table hashes, and only count vendor, department and city cells, where an organisation's name may contain a hashed name by design; they cannot see a hashed name written in another order; rejected: replacing the name inside the description (leaves reordered and truncated forms), blanking only the hashed row's own cells (other rows name the person), hashing every name an id carries (the other table, with no ids, then shows those names in clear), landing each table alone (a payee one table hashes stayed in clear in the other's descriptions: 24 names in 1,242 contract descriptions, 26 in 1,281 once the register kept vouched vendors' descriptions), the later table reading the earlier one's landed hashes (an order the operator must remember, and the earlier table lands without the later one's names), a temporary file for the scrubbed rows (a killed step leaves it behind), typing columns at landing (a bad value would fail the land instead of being counted).
- **Chicago's payments are one pinned query, every row the portal created by the cut-off (`:created_at`), not slices by year:** the whole table is about 476,000 rows and 38 MB as served, a fourteenth of the kit's 512 MiB cap, so one checksum covers it and any one fiscal year is a filter on the landed file; the cut-off is the portal's row-creation time because payments with check dates years old arrive every day, so a check-date cut-off would move daily, and the City reloads the whole table when it rolls payments up, which moves the hash and fails the fetch loudly until it is re-pinned; rejected: one source per year (two dozen checksums for a table that fits one, and the rolled-up and itemised rows of a year would need two filters), raising the byte cap (nothing needs it).
- **A download whose connection drops is run again from the first byte, three attempts in all with a wait of 5 and then 10 seconds, inside the one 30-minute deadline:** Chicago's portal closes some long responses before their end and a rerun completes byte for byte, so a closed connection, a body cut short or a timeout (`httpx.NetworkError`, `RemoteProtocolError`, `TimeoutException`) is retried by a loop in `ingest/fetch.py`, while an error status, a proxy refusal, the byte cap, the deadline and a checksum mismatch fail at once, since each is an answer, not a lost one (a portal's passing 503 is rerun by hand); rejected: httpx's transport `retries` (it retries only connecting, never a body cut short), resuming with a `Range` request (portals' pinned queries need not honour it, and the bytes would come from two responses), a retry library (one more dependency for a ten-line loop).
- **The GLEIF register pinned as one dated golden-copy file, landed for the vendors' legal-address countries (GB, NL, US) without what may name a person:** the publisher writes a dated file once and keeps serving it (files from the register's first years still answer, last modified within days of publishing), so a re-fetch next month verifies, and it publishes no country slices, so the whole-world CSV archive (about 507 MB, 5 GB unzipped) is the smallest fixed file; it fits the kit's 512 MiB cap with about 30 MB to spare, which the newest file's growth of about 4 MB a month uses up in about half a year, so a later re-pin needs the download streamed through the step, not a raised cap; the step (`ingest/gleif/legal_entities.py`) refuses an archive declaring more than 6 GiB unzipped, decompresses and reads the CSV one record at a time in memory, so the unfiltered file never touches disk, writes only the screened records to a private work folder under the landing path, and keeps the names, legal form, country, jurisdiction, category, statuses and last-update date as served text;
