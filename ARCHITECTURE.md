# Architecture

The current design, and each major choice with its reason and what it rejected.
Rewritten in place: it describes the system as it is now.

## Components and seams

Real public documents are fetched, landed and loaded into the warehouse; an open-source ERP posts them into journal lines; layered models turn both into certified data products; governed metrics sit on top; one agent answers from them and another drafts entries a named human approves.
A control plane (identity, tool policy, approval, guardrails, audit, traces, evals, cost caps) spans every agent call.

## Choices

- **Real public data, not generated data:** real records carry the revisions, negative awards, missing identifiers and revised figures that generated data leaves out, and those are what the controls must survive; rejected: generated data (clean by construction, so it proves nothing about mess), a well-known marketplace dataset (its non-commercial licence would bind everything derived from it).
- **Three public entities as the row boundary: City of Chicago (USD), UK Department for Transport (GBP), Dutch central government (EUR):** Chicago brings the only public contract to voucher to payment chain, the UK a second file layout and an April to March year, the Netherlands budget against outturn; together they give three currencies and three fiscal calendars to translate and consolidate; rejected: one entity (no row boundary, no translation), a private company's ledger (none is public).
- **Reference data from its publishers:** ECB reference rates translate currency, the GLEIF register matches vendor names to legal entities, and annual filings from the securities regulator give an independent figure to tie out against; rejected: a public warehouse copy of the filings (stale for years).
- **An open-source ERP (Odoo) as posting engine and write target:** it posts the real documents into journal lines by real accounting rules, loaded as SAP-shaped ledger tables, and is the system the acting agent drafts into; rejected: hand-written posting rules (accounting the lab invents), a vendor ERP (licensed and not free).
- **DuckDB through dbt-duckdb as the local engine until BigQuery takes over:** it runs in process with nothing to start, its dialect is the closest that works to BigQuery's (`QUALIFY`, structs and lists, window functions), and the switch is the dbt profile's target, which `LEDGER_LAB_GCP_PROJECT` selects, with the BigQuery adapter in its own dependency group; DuckDB enforces no row, column or masking rules, so the local access harness enforces them itself and the BigQuery proof stays the real one; dialect gaps are cast away in the model SQL (DuckDB's date truncation returns a timestamp, BigQuery's a date); rejected: a BigQuery emulator (partial dialect coverage, fragile under dbt, and it emulates none of the row and column rules), PostgreSQL (real row security and roles, but the farthest dialect and the largest rewrite at the switch).
- **Fiscal calendars as a period table and a day table built from a seed of each entity's first month, over the fiscal years one dbt var (`fiscal_years`) names, with period status as a seed of one row per period:** each year is twelve calendar months from the entity's first month (January for Chicago and the Dutch government, April for the UK department), labelled by the calendar year it opens in (the UK's 2024-25 is 2024) and numbered from period 1, so a day falls in one period and later models join on entity, year and period; dbt data tests hold the rules (one period per day, contiguous periods, the UK year opening on 1 April and closing on 31 March, a status for every period); a date outside the covered years has no row, so a later model tests each date column with the project's `dates_in_fiscal_calendar` test, which fails where a join would silently drop the row; and dbt-core's own cross-database date spine and date macros keep the SQL unchanged for BigQuery; the Dutch budget moments are versions of one year's budget, not periods a day falls in, so the calendar leaves them out and the budget-against-outturn model carries each moment as a column beside the year; rejected: labelling a year by the year it closes in (the UK writes 2024-25, opening year first), dbt-utils for the date spine (a package download for what dbt-core ships), a case per entity in the SQL (a new entity would be a code change, not a row).
- **Currency is translated by the `translate_currency` macro through the ECB's euro rates, one rate set a day in `fx_rates`: the set of the posting or payment date, the previous business day's when that day is closed (carried at most four days), the cross rate through the euro, or with `constant_rates_on` one named day's set for every row (constant currency); results round once, half away from zero, to two decimal places, and an amount no rate translates stops the query:** the ECB publishes each currency against the euro only, so dollars to pounds is the amount divided by dollars per euro and multiplied by pounds per euro, rounded once so it never drifts a penny from its two legs; `fx_rates` spreads each publication date over the days before the next, so its days follow the published rates whatever years the calendars cover; four days is the longest run of closed days in the published rates (Good Friday to Easter Monday, or 25 and 26 December beside a weekend), so a longer gap, a day before the first rate or after the last, and an unknown or missing currency have no rate, and the macro raises an error naming the currencies and the day (both engines spell it `error(message)` and evaluate it only on the row a `case` sends to it), since a null would vanish from a sum and a caller could forget its test; a null amount stays null; rates are decimals of six places and amounts dbt's `type_numeric` (`numeric(28,6)` on DuckDB), and because DuckDB divides decimals as floats, which misround a half cent (542.05 dollars on 5 April 2024 are exactly 428.865 pounds), the DuckDB quotient is taken exactly in 128-bit integers, while BigQuery divides in BIGNUMERIC, rounding to cents from 38 decimal places (not yet run on BigQuery); the `source_rows` var, one for every fetched source through the `landed_or_sample` macro, reads the landed file by default, so a run with nothing landed fails on the missing file, and the builds in the tests pass `sample` to read committed windows of real rates (25 March to 8 April 2024: a weekend, and Good Friday to Easter Monday; and for the group totals 29 December 2023 and the whole of March 2026; the ECB allows free use with the source cited); rejected: rounding the euro leg first (a cent's drift from the cross), carrying a rate with no limit (a missing week would reuse a stale rate silently), a null for a missing rate with a not_null test each caller adds (a forgotten test lets a sum skip the row), dividing BigQuery's NUMERIC (rounded to 9 places, then to cents, it can round twice near a half cent for amounts with more than 2 decimal places), a day spine from the fiscal calendars (rates outside their years would silently get no row), a package for the fill (a window and a five-row offset in dbt-core do it), the sample as the default (a real run would quietly translate with test fixtures), rates built at test time from a download (`make check` would need the network).
- **The Dutch budget against outturn is one row per budget line, year and kind of amount (`budget_against_outturn`), the figures as the publisher's file serves them: each budget moment a column in euros, the latest moment the file serves for the year as its current budget, named in `current_budget_moment`, the outturn in its own column beside it, and `has_doubtful_zero` on a line the file may serve a zero in place of a figure, over a staging model that types the landed rows (`stg_nl_government__budget_moments`):** served rows of one year, kind, chapter code and article number are one line only on the file's evidence, a non-zero figure repeated across them and none that differs, the line taking each moment's figure once, and otherwise each row is its own line, numbered within the code (`line_in_article`); of the real file's 152 pairs sharing a code, 48 are one article on two rows that repeat the later moments, and the rest stay apart: 40 are chapter `IIB` carrying a chapter `III` (Algemene Zaken) article, 48 are chapter `IX` holding national debt and taxes under one number until 2020, 10 are chapter `L` holding the Nationaal Groeifonds beside Foreign Affairs or Klimaatfonds articles, 4 are chapter `A`, whose name alternates between Mobiliteitsfonds and Infrastructuurfonds, and 2 are the 2024 unallocated reserve, whose rows repeat nothing; the chapter name is left out of the match because chapter `A` is served under both names in one year, and tests fail on a figure repeated across two lines, on a line of two rows with no repeat, on a new pair that neither repeats nor differs, on a served figure no line carries, and on a line that differs from a figure the publisher's annual reports print; a moment with no non-zero amount anywhere in a year is not served in the file for that year (2026's first and second supplementary budgets and outturn, and the September one before 2023), so it is null on every line; a zero at a served moment is the figure as served, which is sometimes a figure the file leaves out (the 2023 second supplementary budget for chapter XII prints 290,715 and 109,767 thousand for articles 16 and 13, where the file serves 0), so a zero between non-zero figures of a line, or a zero draft before non-zero figures at every later moment, sets `has_doubtful_zero`; wrong figures pass through as served and the landed tests count each kind (a 2025 first supplementary figure about three times too high, one over a hundred times its neighbours, an article served under two chapter codes), never correcting them; the current budget is the second supplementary, September supplementary, first supplementary or draft figure, the first the file serves for the year, and the outturn never fills it; amounts are dbt's `type_numeric` in euros, the published thousands times 1,000, never a float; the years are those the fiscal calendar covers for the entity, by a join to `fiscal_periods`; names are trimmed and the two doubly encoded letters the file serves are repaired with `replace`, and a test fails on any new one; the `source_rows` var reads the landed file by default and `sample` a committed set of real landed rows (CC0) in the tests' builds; the file holds budget lines, not payments to anyone (no payee, date or document), so it adds no payments rows; the SQL keeps to dbt's cross-database casts and `concat`, `case`, `count(distinct case ...)` and window functions, so only the source's `external_location` (DuckDB reading the landed Parquet) waits for a BigQuery load (not yet run on BigQuery); rejected: summing the served rows per article code (it doubles the repeated moments, 76 billion euros of 2024's cash outturn), merging rows whose figures merely never differ (it joins the Nationaal Groeifonds to a Klimaatfonds article), the chapter name in the match (an alternating name would split one article), correcting the file's figures (the lab would invent what the publisher did not serve), a zero always read as a real zero (it hides figures the file leaves out), a seed of each year's served moments (a second copy of what the file says, edited at each re-pin), amounts in thousands (every reader must remember the scale, and the other entities' amounts are in units), an intermediate model for the merge (used by nothing else), a payments shape from the outturn lines (it would invent the payees, dates and documents the file does not hold).
- **The group totals are one row per entity, span and basis (`group_totals`), with the count and amount each rule leaves out beside them (`group_totals_left_out`), each row of both with the distinct payees it covers, over one row per figure with its rule in the intermediate layer (`int_group_totals__figures`), in euros at actual and at constant rates, the currency and the constant day one dbt var (`group_totals`):** the entities bring unlike figures, so each row names its figure in `basis`, and a group total is the sum of a span's rows read with their bases: Chicago's dated payments are split into direct vouchers and contract payments because they change in kind (before 2025 almost all dated rows are direct vouchers, the contract payments served as yearly roll-ups; from 2025 most are contract payments), the UK department brings only payments over £25,000 for fiscal 2025, and the Dutch government no payments but a year's gross cash-expenditure outturn of the whole State, its contributions to the budget funds, debt financing and cash management sized in the basis's description and never netted out; a span is the entity's fiscal period, or its fiscal year for a figure with a year and no day (Chicago's yearly roll-ups and the Dutch outturn, about 99% Dutch), never spread over months; the Dutch figure is the cash-expenditure outturn only, so commitments, receipts and every budget moment (the 2025 first supplementary figure the file serves about three times too high among them) never enter, and a year without outturn (2026) is left out; a payment takes its own day's rates through `translate_currency` and a figure with a year and no day the mean of the year's published rates to six places, which equals the ECB's own unrounded yearly average to six places (a pages test holds 2023 to 2025 to it); constant rates are those of 29 December 2023, the ECB's last of calendar 2023; an entity's span whose rates start more than four days after it or end more than four days before it, or holding a dated payment before the first rate or after the last, is left out whole as `no-rate` and counted beside the totals, so no total is partial and a payment past the rates never stops the build, while one inside a gap of the rates still stops the query; the figures sit in the intermediate layer, which no persona reads, because they are one row per payment and several personas may read the product only as aggregates; EUR is the reporting currency because the ECB quotes every currency against it, so only the dollar and pound legs are ever divided; rejected: a group row per month in the table (it would sum unlike bases into one figure with nothing on the row to say so), one Chicago `payments` basis (an average month would jump from about $346M in 2023 to about $849M in 2025 under one label), spreading yearly figures over twelve months (an allocation the sources give no ground for), the Dutch current budget for a year without outturn (a plan, not spend), the year's closing rate for yearly figures (a balance-sheet rate, not the rate the year's flows were made at), dbt model access or groups for the figures (the project uses neither, and the layer already keeps them out of the product).
- **The governed metrics are MetricFlow semantic models and metrics (dbt-metricflow 0.15.0, MetricFlow 0.213.0, beside dbt-core 1.12 and DuckDB 2.0), written in `semantics/metricflow/`, which the dbt project reads as a second model path (`../semantics/metricflow`), each metric's owner, glossary term and role in its own `config.meta`:** MetricFlow reads definitions only from the dbt project's parsed manifest, so the model path keeps them under `semantics/` while dbt parses only that folder, never the glossary or the other files beside it; metric time is `metric_time_spine`, the calendars' `fiscal_spine` macro by day; every metric, the inputs of a ratio or a derived metric included, names as owner the persona who owns its glossary term, the one accountable for the number (the `metric-owner` persona edits definitions by its grant and owns none), and a role, `headline` for a figure to read or `input` for one that only feeds a headline and says so in its description; `semantics/finance_metrics.yaml`, checked by its schema, maps each promised finance metric to its headline metrics or says why it is not definable, and a test holds it to the roles; a test refuses a metric whose owner is missing, unknown, an agent or not its term's owner, whose term the glossary lacks or that has no role, each case planted in the YAML and read back through dbt's parse; `semantics/offline/offline_mf.py` runs `mf` with no network call, since the command asks PyPI for a newer release of itself on every run with no switch to stop it: it turns that check off and ends the run on any connection or name lookup, and every process the tests start for MetricFlow has `semantics/offline/` on its import path, whose `sitecustomize.py` ends it on a network call even without the runner; rejected: the YAML inside `models/` (the definitions would leave `semantics/`), the whole of `semantics/` as a model path (dbt would parse the glossary and every later file as schema files), owners or roles in a file beside the metrics (two places to drift), the `metric-owner` persona as every metric's owner (a custodian, not the person accountable), a metrics layer written by hand, the hosted dbt Semantic Layer (a cloud service outside the lab).
- **Each metric says what the public files let it say:** the contract metrics read the Chicago contract reconciliation, the one definition of a contract's award and paid total, with its break class as a dimension: a contract has a fixed award where the award it is held to is above 0, which counts revisions that net to 0 at their highest award and leaves out a blanket whose every revision is 0, a negative award, a contract whose award in force never rose above 0 and a number only payments carry (it has no award); spend against award is what was paid against those contracts over their awards; remaining commitment is, per such contract, the award not yet paid and never below 0, summed, with what contracts were paid over their award as its own metric (`paid_over_award`, the reconciliation's positive differences over the same contracts) instead of netted, and a contract past its end date still in it unless filtered on its latest end date (`contract__last_ends_on`); by `metric_time` the contract metrics group contracts by approval cohort, the day of their first revision, which the dimension's name (`approval_cohort`) says, since the reconciliation holds totals, not balances at a date; the duplicate-payment rate, by rows and by amount, is Chicago's rows flagged `is_repeat`, the publisher serving a payment twice, since nothing in the files shows the City paying twice, and Chicago only, the one entity with a rule for repeats; constant-currency spend is the group totals at constant rates as a derived metric that is a figure only where the rows it sums share one basis, so no query adds the Dutch outturn to Chicago's payments, and whose whole-year figures are measured on a year-only time dimension, so a query by month or day is refused instead of placing a year in January, and the period start of a whole year's row is empty (`span` names the row's kind); days to pay is not defined, since Chicago's payments serve only a check date and the UK spend only a payment date, so no file holds the invoice or voucher date it needs, and MetricFlow defines a metric only over a model that exists (the ERP's postings, not built yet); rejected: contract approval to payment as days to pay (another measure under its name), remaining commitment netted with overpayments (it can go below 0 and hides overpayment), a second per-contract award and paid total beside the reconciliation's (two definitions to drift), one figure across bases (adds unlike things), a whole year's figure placed in its first month (a month it does not belong to).
- **Native GCP services only where free, a stand-in behind the same seam otherwise:** BigQuery enforces row, column and masking rules itself, which is the point; the platform's catalog, lineage and native data agent are used only once they prove free; rejected: stand-ins for everything (they prove nothing about the real platform's enforcement), paid managed services (the lab spends $0.00).
- **Personas as service identities that tests impersonate, with a mapping table in place of a group directory:** each persona id is a service account id, `platform/gcp/` declares one account per persona read from `access/personas.yaml` itself, with no project role but the one that runs a query, and the agent account may mint tokens on each of them and nowhere else; a test acts as a persona and asserts what the warehouse returns, on BigQuery by impersonating `<persona>@<project>.iam.gserviceaccount.com`, and the persona to entity to data product table in `access/` plays the directory's role; rejected: a group directory (needs an organisation and an administrator the lab does not have), personal accounts (need real people and email addresses in a public repo).
- **A persona on DuckDB is a database file named after it, compiled from the catalogues by [`access/compile.py`](access/compile.py) to hold only what the persona may see, opened read-only with no file beyond it reachable, no extension to load, and its configuration locked:** DuckDB has no users, grants, row policies or masking, so a connection reads whatever it can open;
